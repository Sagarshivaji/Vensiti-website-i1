Why platform teams are betting on pull-based delivery

8 min read · Vensiti DevOps & Cloud practice

Most deploy pipelines still work the same way they did a decade ago: CI builds an artifact, then pushes it into production using credentials it holds itself. That model is quietly becoming the riskiest part of the stack.

The problem with push

A push-based pipeline needs write access to production to do its job — which means every CI job, every plugin, and every contributor to that pipeline's configuration is a potential path into your cluster. When something goes wrong, the blast radius is the entire deploy credential's permission set, not just the one service being shipped.

It also means production drifts from what's in git the moment someone runs a manual `kubectl apply` to fix an incident at 2am. Six months later, nobody remembers which changes are documented and which only exist as tribal knowledge.

What pull-based delivery changes

In a pull model, an agent running inside the cluster — ArgoCD and Flux are the common choices — continuously compares the live state against what's declared in git, and reconciles the difference itself. Nothing outside the cluster ever holds a credential capable of writing to it.

Rollback stops being a runbook and becomes a git revert.

That single change has a compounding effect: rollback stops being a runbook and becomes a git revert, drift gets caught and corrected automatically instead of discovered during an incident, and every change to production has a commit, an author and a diff attached to it by construction — not by discipline.

What we've seen work

The teams that adopt this cleanly don't try to migrate everything at once. They pick one non-critical service, stand up the pull agent alongside the existing pipeline, and run both in parallel until the team trusts the git-as-source-of-truth model before cutting the old pipeline's write access.

That staged approach is also where most of the value of a two-week assessment shows up: naming which services are safe to move first, and which ones have enough undocumented manual intervention that they need to be untangled before pull-based delivery will actually hold.

The hidden costs of a bad tech hire — and how to avoid them

6 min read · Vensiti Talent practice

The obvious cost of a bad technical hire is the salary paid before it unwinds. The real cost is almost always several multiples larger, and most of it never shows up on the same line item.

The real price tag

Start with the visible costs: recruiter or agency fees, onboarding time, equipment, and — if it gets that far — severance. Then add the costs that don't get tracked against the hire at all: the senior engineer who spent three months mentoring someone who then left, the sprint velocity that quietly dropped while the team covered gaps, and the project that shipped six weeks late because a critical piece was rewritten by someone else after the fact.

Team morale is the cost that compounds the longest. A hire the team doesn't trust technically erodes confidence in the hiring process itself — and the next open req gets more scrutiny, more delay, and more caution than it needed.

Where the vetting process usually breaks

Most technical hiring still leans on keyword-matched resumes and a recruiter screen that can't actually evaluate the work. By the time a hiring manager sees a candidate, the technical bar has already been set by someone who isn't qualified to set it.

By the time a hiring manager sees a candidate, the technical bar was set by someone who couldn't evaluate it.

What we do differently

Every candidate we send is interviewed by an engineer who does the same job — not a generalist recruiter working from a checklist. That's why we send three curated profiles instead of thirty resumes: the filtering already happened, by someone qualified to filter.

And because the vetting is real, we back it: a 90-day replacement window at no additional fee, and a 97% retention rate at twelve months across placements. The guarantee isn't generosity — it's a bet we're comfortable making because the process in front of it actually works.

From senior dev to staff engineer: the skills that actually matter

7 min read · Vensiti Talent practice

Most senior engineers assume the next step up is more of what got them there: better code, faster delivery, deeper expertise in their stack. That's necessary but it isn't what actually moves the needle at staff level.

Influence without authority

A staff engineer's output isn't measured in commits — it's measured in decisions other people make correctly because of something you did. That means writing the design doc that gets three teams aligned before code is written, not the one that documents a decision after the fact.

It also means being willing to be the person who says a plan won't scale, in a room where saying that is uncomfortable, and being right often enough that people start asking before they commit to the plan.

Systems thinking, not ticket thinking

Senior engineers are excellent at solving the problem in front of them. Staff engineers are expected to notice when the problem in front of them is a symptom of a problem three teams over — and to have the credibility to raise it before it becomes an incident.

The promotion case writes itself when the org can point to a decision that wouldn't have gone well without you.

How to show it, not just have it

The skills above are invisible unless there's a paper trail. Write the design docs. Mentor deliberately, not incidentally — and make sure the people you mentor can point to what changed because of it. Take the unglamorous cross-team project nobody else wants, because that's usually where the org-level leverage actually is.

The promotion case writes itself when the org can point to a specific decision, incident avoided, or team unblocked that wouldn't have gone the way it did without you. That's a very different portfolio than "shipped a lot of code" — and it's the one staff-level interview loops are actually screening for.

Why your cloud bill keeps climbing, and what to do about it

9 min read · Vensiti DevOps & Cloud practice

Cloud bills almost never spike from one dramatic mistake. They creep — a few percent a month, quietly, until someone in finance asks a question nobody on the engineering side can answer.

The usual suspects

Orphaned resources are the most common: load balancers and volumes left behind after a service was decommissioned, staging environments nobody remembers spinning up, snapshots kept indefinitely by a default retention policy nobody set on purpose.

Over-provisioning is the second: instance sizes chosen once, during a launch under time pressure, and never revisited once real traffic data existed to right-size them. Add unmetered egress between services that could have shared a region, and the bill becomes a sum of a dozen small decisions nobody owns.

Why nobody notices until finance does

Cost visibility usually lives in a billing dashboard nobody on the engineering team opens, tagged — if tagged at all — inconsistently enough that attributing spend to a team or service takes more effort than anyone has time for. Without ownership, "someone should look at this" never turns into someone actually looking at it.

Without ownership, "someone should look at this" never turns into someone actually looking at it.

A two-week fix

A cost and capacity review doesn't start with a mandate to cut spend — it starts with a line-item breakdown of what's actually driving the bill, with the over-provisioned resources named specifically, not just flagged as "high cost." That distinction matters: a list a team can act on beats a dashboard they have to interpret.

Most engagements find the same shape of answer: a handful of specific, named changes that cut real cost without touching anything customer-facing, plus a tagging and ownership convention that keeps the next six months of creep from happening the same way.

A hiring manager's guide to technical screens that actually work

5 min read · Vensiti Talent practice

Most technical screens test whether a candidate can perform under artificial pressure, not whether they can do the job. Those are different skills, and optimizing for the wrong one filters out exactly the engineers you want.

Whiteboard algorithms measure the wrong skill

Live-coding an algorithm from memory, with someone watching, correlates weakly with day-to-day engineering performance — and it disproportionately screens out strong engineers who don't interview well under that specific kind of pressure, which has nothing to do with whether they can ship reliable software.

What to ask instead

A real code review exercise — reviewing an actual (anonymized) pull request from your own codebase — tells you how a candidate thinks about tradeoffs, edge cases and communication, with none of the artificial time pressure. A debugging exercise on a real, reproducible bug tests the skill the job actually requires far more directly than an algorithm question does.

A system design conversation is a conversation, not an interrogation — and it should feel like one.

A system design conversation, run as an actual back-and-forth rather than an interrogation, reveals how someone reasons about scale, failure modes and tradeoffs — which is precisely the skill that separates a senior hire from a junior one, and precisely the skill a leetcode-style question can't measure at all.

Who should run the screen

The single highest-leverage change most hiring processes can make is having the technical screen run by a senior engineer in the same discipline as the role — not a generalist recruiter working from a scorecard they didn't write and can't deviate from. A peer knows which follow-up question actually distinguishes real experience from a memorized answer.

What separates a 90-day retention hire from a bad one

6 min read · Vensiti Talent practice

Most hiring failures aren't visible in the first interview loop — they show up in the first ninety days, when the gap between what was promised and what was real becomes impossible to ignore.

It starts before day one

The single biggest predictor of a fast churn isn't a skills gap — it's a scoping gap. When the intake conversation is a generic job description instead of a real conversation about the actual work, the candidate who accepts the offer is accepting a role that doesn't exist. The mismatch surfaces in week three, not in the interview.

A forty-minute intake call focused on the actual work — the specific systems, the specific team friction points, the specific reason the role is open — sets an expectation that survives contact with reality. That's a cheap fix for the most expensive failure mode in hiring.

The technical screen filters skill, not fit

A candidate can be technically excellent and still be a fast-churn hire if nobody checked whether the way they like to work matches how the team actually operates. Skill and fit are different axes, and most processes only screen for one of them.

A candidate can be technically excellent and still be a fast-churn hire if the fit was never checked.

What we track that others don't

We measure retention at twelve months, not just whether an offer was accepted — because an accepted offer that churns in four months isn't a successful placement, whatever the invoice says. That number currently sits at 97% across our placements, and it's the metric the whole vetting process is built around protecting, from the intake call through the 90-day guarantee.
